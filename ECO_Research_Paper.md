# Quantifying War-Time Environmental Damage: A Difference-in-Differences Analysis of the Kakhovka Dam Destruction

**Sakshi D. Maske**

*Independent Geospatial Researcher*

## Abstract

International law organisations are actively looking into declaring "ecocide" — mass environmental destruction — as a prosecutable international crime, and in September 2024 Vanuatu, Fiji and Samoa submitted a proposed amendment to the Rome Statute. Existing satellite-based assessments of conflict-related environmental damage have predominantly relied on descriptive or qualitative change detection and generally do not establish event-specific statistical attribution, in part because prior environmental trends are not easily differentiated from harm caused by conflict. In an attempt to address this specific gap, I estimate the association between the Kakhovka Dam's destruction and vegetation change in Ukraine using a Difference-in-Differences causal-inference framework, comparing monthly NDVI in Kherson Oblast against a four-county control panel in Romania (Tulcea, Galați, Brăila and Constanța) chosen for comparable pre-conflict river-delta, steppe and coastal ecology along the Danube/Black Sea corridor but unaffected by the dam's destruction.

This paper reports a corrected version of that analysis, and states plainly what the correction changed. An earlier version of the underlying NDVI extraction queried Sentinel Hub using each zone's rectangular bounding box rather than its true administrative (GADM) polygon, and filtered cloud contamination only at the whole-scene level rather than per pixel. Re-extracting the same data against the true polygon geometry with pixel-level Sentinel-2 Scene Classification masking (excluding cloud, cloud-shadow, cirrus and snow pixels) is the specification reported throughout this paper, and it materially weakens the headline result: the estimated differential is now −0.0747 NDVI (95% CI [−0.153, 0.003]; HAC p = 0.060) — directionally consistent with the originally-computed, bounding-box-based estimate (−0.0703, p = 0.022) but no longer significant at the conventional 5% level. The broad-window placebo test using a counterfactual pre-event date still returns a clean, near-zero, non-significant result (+0.0051, p = 0.882), and the same directional effect carries through an independent four-county panel (−0.0661, HAC p = 0.059). The more consequential change is in an exact randomization-inference check across the five geographic units available to this design (Section 4.6): under the corrected data, Kherson is no longer the single most extreme unit under either a one-sided or two-sided ranking (best-case exact p = 0.40, versus the originally-reported 0.20, which was already the best score obtainable with only five units) — two of the four Romanian control counties, Brăila and Constanța, show comparable-or-larger effects of their own under the identical placebo-in-space procedure, and a companion check with Kherson excluded from the panel entirely (Section 4.8) confirms both counties have their own statistically detectable post-June-2023 divergence, unconnected to the dam. I also checked, and report, whether this weakening could be explained away as noise from a handful of especially low-coverage months in the corrected data; dropping them does not recover the original result, and if anything weakens it slightly further (Section 4.10). The honest reading is that Kherson's NDVI shows a real, directionally consistent decline after June 2023 relative to its main control zone, but this design — with only five geographic units in total — cannot establish that decline as statistically distinguishable from variation already present among the control zones on their own. Both the originally-computed and the corrected estimates, and the full reasoning connecting them, are reported here, because a statistically sound method for this kind of claim has to survive exactly this kind of correction, not stop at whichever version of the analysis happened to look cleanest first.

**Keywords**: ecocide, remote sensing, causal inference, Difference-in-Differences, war crimes, satellite evidence, Kakhovka Dam

## 1. Introduction

Flooding of hundreds of square kilometers of downstream floodplain occurred after the Kakhovka Dam on the Dnipro River in Ukraine was destroyed on 6 June 2023, filling to capacity an 18.2 km³ reservoir, one of the largest in Europe. The evidential techniques available to measure ecocide are still fairly immature compared to the possible legal framework underway to prosecute ecocide.

Doing this with explicitly quantified statistical confidence, I ask whether, in the case of the Kakhovka Dam destruction, a causal-inference framework — which is already a well-established tool in the evaluation of policies and programs — can be sufficiently adapted to distinguish the Kakhovka Dam destruction's specific environmental impact from the inherently elevated baseline level of an active conflict region.

## 2. Literature Review

### 2.1 “Ecocide” in the Law: Emerging Legal Recognition

Today, the process of claiming ecocide as a prosecutable international crime is gaining tremendous momentum in the last few years. The seminal proposal for the inclusion of the definition of 'ecocide' in the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court came in September 2024, when Vanuatu, Fiji and Samoa jointly submitted a proposed amendment for consideration, Amandragment IV, which restates a definition formulated by an independent expert panel convened by the Stop Ecocide Foundation in 2021. This momentum has also continued with respect to the domestic legislation: Belgium is the first European nation to legislate on ecocide at both national and international levels, while proposal for the legislation in Mexico, Italy, the Netherlands, Brazil and the United Kingdom moves forward. It's a piece of an enforcing legal system that's changing quickly, and one that requires evidence-based methodologies that can help prosecute such cases, which I am trying to meet head-on in what follows.

### 2.2 Satellite Imagery in International Legal Proceedings: Persistent Methodological Questions

The body of literature on the use of satellite imagery for courtroom applications consistently points to a particular gap for the use of satellite imagery in courts that remains unresolved – the lack of accepted forensic standards and methods. Satellite imagery has so far been accepted as evidence to support witness evidence in the ICC, but it has not been in itself accepted as dispositive evidence of mass atrocities as yet, in part due to the fact that much of the analysis remains largely expert –meaning experts still interpret images of sightings in such a qualitative way that there is no agreed point of departure. Analysis of the criteria for making data from satellite observations legally useful (operational feasibility, the reliability of the data, and the legal admissibility) reveals that data reliability is a continuing liability, a strand that my causal-inference design attempts to address through explicit stratification of a meaningful treatment effect from background noise and pre-existing trends.

### 2.3 Existing Geospatial Assessment of the Kakhovka Event

The most pertinent prior work involves a recently published geospatial review of Ukraine's environmental impacts resulting from the war, which reviewed the Kakhovka Dam case-study site along with other sites of interest using multi-temporal satellite observations. That evaluation was done with visual interpretation and comparative analysis of pre/post photographs purposely without attribution of direct cause and effect association with the military operations, but which called for future study of developing standardized, quantitative indicators. I decided to meet this call for this event by applying a Difference-in-Differences (DiD), placebo validation, and event study robustness analysis using matched control-zone comparison that had not yet been used in this case.

### 2.4 Environmental Security and Peacebuilding Perspectives

Ecocide and war-time environmental harm are not only a legal question but also a security one. Killean (2025) traces how the concept of ecocide has itself evolved alongside changing understandings of war, arguing that environmental destruction in conflict is increasingly read not as a side effect of fighting but as a strategic or foreseeable consequence of it — a framing directly relevant to the Kakhovka Dam's destruction, an act with an identifiable date and an identifiable downstream environmental signature. Krampe, Kreutz and Ide (2025) show, across a broader set of conflicts, that armed conflict causes environmental harms that persist well beyond the fighting itself, and argue for the same kind of evidence-based, quantitative documentation of that harm that this study attempts for a single case. Read together, this literature suggests that a statistically grounded estimate of conflict-attributable vegetation loss is relevant not only to future legal proceedings (Section 2.1) but also to the environmental-security and post-war-recovery literature, which has so far lacked comparable causal-inference-based case evidence for this specific event.

Treatment zone (Kherson Oblast, Ukraine) and four-county control panel (Tulcea, Galați, Brăila and Constanța, Romania) included in this study. ### 3.1 Study Design

I employed a Difference-in-Differences design, comparing the treatment group (upstream, the floodplain of the dam is in Kherson Oblast, Ukraine) with a four-county control panel in Romania (Tulcea, Galați, Brăila and Constanța) with comparable pre-conflict river-delta, steppe and coastal ecosystems and non-combatant status. The county of Tulcea whose Danube Delta borders is used as the primary control for the headline specification (Section 4.2) and the full four-count panel as a control for the robustness checks reported in Section 4.5.

### 3.3 Causal Model

For seasonal vegetation cycle control, I adapted the month fixed effects approach before regressing a Difference-in-Differences between indices before and after 6 June 2023 between treatment and control zones. A placebo test (however, an event that did not actually occur; a counterfactual treatment date of June 2022) and a quarterly programmed event study (whether the effects were truly centered around the true event date as opposed to being spread out over the next quarter) were used for validation.

A methodological correction to the underlying NDVI extraction, applied throughout this paper, is documented here rather than only in a changelog. An earlier version of the pipeline (`download_ndvi.py`, `download_ndvi_control_zones.py`) queried Sentinel Hub using each zone's rectangular bounding box — derived from, but not identical to, its GADM administrative polygon — and filtered cloud contamination only at the scene level (`maxCloudCoverage` ≤40% per image, before aggregation). Both limitations are corrected in the version reported here: `export_zone_geometries.py` and `download_ndvi_polygon.py` query the true, simplified GADM polygon for each zone directly, and the evalscript adds a per-pixel mask on the Sentinel-2 Scene Classification (SCL) band, excluding no-data, saturated/defective, cloud-shadow, cloud (medium/high probability), cirrus and snow/ice pixels before computing each month's mean. Because a polygon covers less area than its own bounding box, Sentinel Hub's Statistics API reports a correspondingly lower ceiling on valid-pixel coverage per zone under the corrected geometry — confirmed directly by comparing each zone's polygon-to-bounding-box area ratio (≈43–66% depending on zone) against the observed maximum valid-pixel fraction in the corrected data, which match closely. This is the expected, correct behaviour of switching from a bounding box to a true polygon, not a new data-quality problem in itself; Section 4.10 checks directly whether the resulting lower average valid-pixel coverage is nonetheless distorting the headline result.

The primary specification focuses only on two geographic units (Kherson, Tulcea) taken over time (across time), so cluster-robust standard errors — the standard correction in panel designs with many different geographic units — have no meaning; if there is only one cluster, cluster-robust inference is not possible. Because of serial correlation effects present within the data, I therefore employ Newey–West heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors (Newey & West, 1987) in the primary specification—a standard solution employed when dealing with a few long time series. Unless indicated, all reported p-values and confidence intervals are HAC-corrected; I report the classical OLS statistics along with them in Section 4.4 for comparison. I report the pooled and per-zone estimates that I ran for the same design against the entire 4-county panel, and cluster-robust standard errors become mathematically defined (albeit on the very edge, at 5 clusters — I report them with HAC for comparison.

### 3.4 Pre-Treatment Covariate Balance

0.3205 in Kherson. 0.3156 in Tulcea (both under the corrected polygon+SCL extraction described in Section 3.3). In order to have a convincing Difference-in-Differences design, the treatment and control areas should be similar before (not after) the treatment, so in fact I tested this directly. During the pre-period (January 2022 to May 2023), these averages break down as Kherson, n=17 months, SD=0.079, and Tulcea, n=17 months, SD=0.121 — a gap the two-sample (Welch) t-test finds statistically indistinguishable from zero (p=0.888). The estimated baseline level difference (in the presence of the fixed month effects) between zones (the main effect term of the DiD regression) is 0.0049 NDVI, 95% CI [−0.046, 0.056], HAC p=0.846, and once again is not significant. I report here this variability owing to the seasonal amplitude of the runoff of Tulcea (0.333) being significantly greater than the one for Kherson (0.200); this is not exactly analogous to the violation of parallel pre-trends and I checked for this violation explicitly in the event study in Section 4.3.

### 4.2 Vegetation Impact

<p align="center">
  <img src="outputs/plots/ndvi_comparison.png" width="700">
</p>

The figure presents the monthly mean NDVI trends for 2022 to 2024 in the treatment region (Kherson Oblast, Ukraine) and the main control region (Tulcea County, Romania). There is a visible reduction in vegetation greenness in the treatment region relative to the control region around the time of the Kakhovka Dam's destruction in June 2023 — a descriptive pattern that motivates the formal DiD estimation below, not yet the causal estimate itself.

The Difference-in-Differences model, using the corrected polygon+SCL NDVI extraction (Section 3.3), estimates that NDVI in Kherson declined relative to Tulcea by −0.0747 (95% CI [−0.153, 0.003], R² = 0.703). Under HAC-robust standard errors this is p = 0.060 — just outside the conventional 5% threshold — and p = 0.013 under classical (non-robust) standard errors, the same direction of HAC widening the interval relative to classical SEs seen throughout this paper, consistent with positive serial correlation in monthly NDVI. The broad-window placebo test, with June 2022 as a counterfactual treatment date, returns a near-zero, non-significant coefficient (+0.0051, 95% CI [−0.066, 0.076], HAC p = 0.882): the placebo check itself still passes cleanly, meaning whatever is producing the real-date estimate above is not present at an arbitrary earlier date, even though the real-date estimate itself is now only marginal.

From a practical point of view, the coefficient −0.0747 corresponds to approximately 23% of the pre-treatment mean NDVI value of Kherson (0.3205, Section 3.4) — a scale comparison, not a claim that "vegetation fell by 23%": the DiD coefficient is a differential relative to the control zone's own trend, not a direct physical measurement of the treatment zone's change. This is provided to serve as a scale reference for NDVI as a spectral index; it is not a physical measurement of biomass or land area lost, and this study is not aimed at converting NDVI data to hectares or tonnes of vegetation without field calibration data, since this was beyond the scope of the study. Because the estimate itself does not clear the conventional significance threshold under the primary specification's own preferred (HAC) standard errors, this scale comparison should be read as descriptive of the point estimate, not as evidence of a confidently established effect of that size — see Sections 4.6 and 4.8 for the checks that most directly qualify that confidence.

### 4.3 Event Study and a Disclosed Limitation

The results of a quarterly event study, re-estimated on the corrected polygon+SCL NDVI (Section 3.3), show significant negative impacts during the treatment quarter (HAC p<0.0001), the following quarter (HAC p=0.0005), one year post-treatment (HAC p<0.0001), and in a pre-treatment quarter (summer 2022, HAC p<0.0001). All four of these quarters were already flagged as significant in the original, bounding-box-based analysis, and remain so — in fact more strongly so — under the corrected data. As before, the pre-treatment quarter signal is attributed to the fact that much of Kherson was already an active conflict zone, with a major liberation operation under way, well before the dam's destruction, rather than to a violation of this design's pre-trend assumption specifically around June 2023.

The narrowed-baseline specification (restricting to January 2023 onward) shows a larger effect under the corrected data (−0.1497, 95% CI [−0.226, −0.074], HAC p=0.0004; classical p=0.0032) than the original bounding-box estimate (−0.1384, HAC p=0.0001). Its own placebo test — a fake mid-window split date within the same narrow period — behaves differently under the corrected data than it did originally: the corrected narrowed-baseline placebo returns −0.1098 (95% CI [−0.231, 0.012], HAC p=0.0693), no longer significant, versus the original bounding-box placebo's HAC p=0.001, which was reported as a decisive validation failure for that specification. This is one of the few places in this revision where the corrected data behaves better, not worse, than the original: the specific concern that the narrowed-baseline result might just reflect an arbitrary split within an already-short, highly-serially-correlated window is less severe under the corrected data — though the small sample (n=10) means this specific comparison should not be over-read in either direction. Both the narrowed-baseline DiD and its placebo remain a secondary specification illustrating the pre-treatment-quarter problem, not an independently validated primary result; that role belongs to the primary two-zone specification in Section 4.2, which is the specification that weakened materially under this correction.

### 4.5 Multi-Control Robustness Check — A Four-County Panel

The main specification (Section 4.2) is based on the control zone Tulcea. To support this selection, and as a further robustness check, the same causal design was applied to the same period (January 2022–November 2024), to the same geography (the four counties of Tulcea, Galați, Brăila, and Constanța), and to the same corrected polygon+SCL dataset used throughout this paper.

The effect is still directionally present with Kherson against all four controls, the sign of did_term unchanged, though both the HAC and cluster-robust cross-check are now weaker than originally reported: did_term = −0.0661 (HAC 95% CI [−0.135, 0.003], p = 0.059; cluster-robust by zone, 95% CI [−0.127, −0.005], p = 0.034) — a reduction in significance similar in direction and size to the primary two-zone specification (Section 4.2), not a reversal or collapse to zero. Passing the same fake June 2022 treatment date through the placebo test on the same four-control panel still returns a clean, non-significant result under HAC (coefficient +0.0421, HAC p = 0.338), though the cluster-robust cross-check for this same placebo is now borderline (p = 0.078) rather than clearly clean (the original bounding-box placebo's cluster p was 0.216) — consistent with cluster-robust standard errors being the less reliable of the two cross-checks at only 5 clusters (Section 3.3), rather than a sign the placebo is failing under its own preferred HAC specification.

Brăila and Galați hold up best individually; Tulcea is now the more marginal of the pairwise comparisons; Constanța continues to show essentially no relationship, and Section 4.8 gives that null result a more concrete explanation than an open ecological question, and shows it is not a purely benign one.

This result is accompanied by two forthright disclaimers. First, that's the minimum number of clusters required for cluster-robust inference to be even mathematically defined, let alone one that is large enough for the standard error characteristics resulting from the asymptotic theory to be reliable — the suggested rule of thumb is that we should have 30-40+ clusters or more, and the few-cluster cases are a documented source of understated standard errors — so the cluster-robust p-values reported here should be understood as a cross-check on proper panel size, rather than being a replacement. Second, running the same four-county panel in a quarterly event study (`event_study_multi_control.py`) again extends this approximation beyond its useful range: with about 24 parameters (twelve month dummies, treatment, and about eleven event coefficients for the quarters), estimated from five clusters, the covariance matrix returns a rank of 4 instead of 24 — not close — and standard errors on individual quarter coefficients of around 1e-16, an artifact of too few clusters rather than real precision. The HAC specification of the same panel-based event study is reported instead, and under the corrected data it shows the same qualitative pattern as before: the treatment-quarter effect is not significant when pooled across four heterogeneous controls (HAC p = 0.278, versus p<0.0001 in the two-zone design), while the one-year-later effect remains significant (HAC p = 0.0001). This is shown as a real complication, not one that can be harmonized out of the picture; the finer-grained timing story by quarter is even less settled than the already-marginal pooled DiD statistic, not more.

### 4.6 Placebo-in-Space: A Randomization-Inference Check

Section 4.5 asks whether the Kherson effect survives against each control zone individually, with Kherson always held fixed as the treated unit. That is not quite the same question a skeptical reader would ask with only five geographic units on the table: if one of the four Romanian counties, rather than Kherson, had been the one assigned "treated" status at the real June 2023 cutoff, would it have shown an effect just as large? Cluster-robust or HAC p-values computed from five clusters cannot rule this out on their own (Conley & Taber, 2011); an exact randomization test can, so I ran one directly, on the corrected polygon+SCL data.

Each of the five zones was assigned "treated" status in turn, against the other four as its control panel, using the same real post-June-2023 cutoff. Under the corrected data, the ranking changes materially from the originally-reported (bounding-box) result. Kherson's own coefficient (did_term = −0.0661, HAC p = 0.059) is no longer the most extreme of the five: Brăila, assigned fake-treated status, returns +0.0935 (HAC p = 0.015) — larger in absolute size and more significant than Kherson's own coefficient, though opposite in sign — and Constanța returns −0.0893 (HAC p = 0.048), a larger negative coefficient, in the same direction as Kherson's decline, and itself conventionally significant. Tulcea and Galați, the remaining two placebo assignments, come back smaller and non-significant (+0.027 and +0.035). Ranked one-sided (most-negative-first), Kherson is 2nd of 5 (exact randomization p = 2/5 = 0.40); ranked two-sided (largest absolute effect first), Kherson is 3rd of 5 (exact randomization p = 3/5 = 0.60). Both are materially worse than the originally-reported rank of 1st of 5 (exact p = 0.20), which was already the best possible score obtainable in a five-unit panel.

This is the single most consequential change this correction makes to the paper's causal claim. The point is not that Kherson's coefficient became small — it did not; −0.0661 is a similar magnitude to the originally-reported −0.0600 — but that, once the panel's NDVI is extracted correctly, at least one and arguably two of the four Romanian control counties independently show a comparable or larger shift around the same real-world date, for reasons that have nothing to do with the Kakhovka Dam. With only five clusters, randomization inference cannot distinguish Kherson's post-event decline from Brăila's or Constanța's own idiosyncratic movement over the same window. Section 4.8 investigates directly what is actually happening in those two counties.

### 4.7 Multiple-Testing Correction for the Event Study

The quarterly event study in Section 4.3 tests eleven separate quarter coefficients against one pre-treatment reference quarter. Testing that many coefficients raises the ordinary multiple-comparisons concern: at a nominal α=0.05 per test, the chance of at least one false positive across eleven tests is well above 5% by construction, so the raw per-quarter p-values in Section 4.3 should not be read as though each were the only test run.

Applying a Bonferroni correction (family-wise α=0.05 across 11 tests, corrected threshold p<0.00455) leaves four of the eleven quarters standing under the corrected data: the pre-treatment quarter (summer 2022, p<0.0001), the treatment quarter itself (p<0.0001), the following quarter (p=0.0005), and the one-year-post-treatment quarter (p<0.0001) — a stronger result than the original bounding-box analysis, where only two of these four quarters survived Bonferroni. Applying the less conservative Benjamini–Hochberg false-discovery-rate procedure (Benjamini & Hochberg, 1995) at q=0.05 recovers the same four quarters, plus one additional quarter five quarters after treatment (p=0.0094), which was not previously reported as a distinct finding.

The practical read: under the corrected data, more of the quarterly event-study coefficients survive multiple-testing correction than under the original bounding-box data — an improvement that sits in some tension with the primary pooled specification (Section 4.2), which weakened under the same correction. This is not a contradiction: the event study and the pooled DiD use different HAC lag lengths (1 versus 3) and test different contrasts (quarter-specific versus a single pre/post average), so they can move in different directions under the same underlying data correction. This paper should therefore not be read as uniformly weaker in every respect after the correction — some checks got noisier, this one got sharper — and neither direction should be assumed to generalize to the other without checking, which is the reason each check in this paper is reported on its own terms rather than folded into a single headline number.

### 4.8 Spillover Check Among Control Zones

Section 6 argues that spatial spillover into the control counties is unlikely, but only on distance grounds (~350 km, across an international border) — a plausibility argument, not a test. I ran the direct test the existing data allows: with Kherson excluded from the panel entirely, each of the four Romanian counties was in turn assigned "treated" status against the other three, at the same real June 2023 cutoff. If the war's regional effects had spilled into the ostensibly unaffected controls, at least one of these four control-only comparisons should show a shift even with Kherson removed.

Under the corrected polygon+SCL data, this check comes back materially different from the original bounding-box result. Tulcea (did_term = +0.0116, HAC p = 0.742) and Galați (+0.0192, p = 0.567) still come back clean, with no detectable shift relative to the other controls. But both Brăila (+0.0821, HAC p = 0.027) and Constanța (−0.1129, HAC p = 0.020) now show a significant shift relative to the other three — Brăila was only borderline in the original analysis (p=0.069), and Constanța's already-significant result becomes more so.

This gives the Section 4.6 finding a concrete mechanism: Brăila and Constanța are not artifacts of the placebo-in-space randomization draw, they are counties that genuinely moved on their own, independent of Kherson, sometime around the same June 2023 window, for reasons this design does not identify (disrupted Black Sea shipping and regional agricultural-market effects are plausible candidates, but this test establishes that a shift happened, not why). Because the primary specification (Section 4.2) uses Tulcea — the one control county that passes this check cleanly — this does not mean the primary specification's own control zone is compromised in the same way. It does mean the four-county pooled panel (Section 4.5) and the placebo-in-space ranking (Section 4.6) are both visibly affected by two of their four candidate control counties having independent post-event dynamics of their own, a materially larger share of the control panel than the original bounding-box analysis suggested (which flagged only Constanța, not Brăila).

### 4.9 Specification Robustness: Lag Length and Functional Form

Two further analyst choices in the primary specification (Section 3.3) were made once and reported without themselves being stress-tested: the Newey–West lag length, and the linear functional form of the outcome variable. Both were re-examined directly against the primary Kherson-vs-Tulcea model, on the corrected data.

**Lag length.** The main specification uses maxlags=3. Re-estimating the identical model at maxlags=1 through 6 leaves the did_term coefficient unchanged, as it must (HAC affects only the standard errors, not the point estimate), and its p-value ranges from 0.027 (maxlags=1) to 0.063 (maxlags=4) — crossing the conventional 0.05 threshold depending on which lag length is chosen: maxlags=1, 2 and 6 fall just under 0.05, maxlags=3, 4 and 5 fall just over it. Unlike the original bounding-box analysis, where the lag-length choice did not affect the qualitative conclusion at all (p range 0.011–0.026, always clearly significant), the corrected data's result sits close enough to the boundary that the lag-length choice can flip the significance verdict. This is disclosed directly rather than quietly picking the lag length that produces the more favourable answer: this paper's primary reported specification uses maxlags=3 (the original, pre-registered choice, unchanged by this revision) for consistency, not because it happens to give the most favourable p-value.

**Functional form.** The primary specification models raw NDVI linearly. Re-estimating the identical specification on log(NDVI) instead returns a similarly-signed effect (a roughly 25% proportional decline) but a weaker p-value (0.081, versus 0.060 for the linear specification) — both readings are now outside the conventional 0.05 threshold, whereas in the original bounding-box analysis the linear specification cleared it (p=0.022–0.026) and only the log specification did not (p=0.055). The direction of the sensitivity — log weaker than linear — is the same as originally reported, and for the same likely reason (cloud-contaminated near-zero-NDVI months become proportionally larger swings once log-transformed), but under the corrected data neither functional form clears conventional significance, which is a more consequential version of the same caveat than before.

### 4.10 Sensitivity to Low-Coverage Months

Section 3.3 explains why the corrected polygon+SCL extraction structurally lowers the ceiling on valid-pixel coverage per zone relative to the original bounding-box extraction — a true administrative polygon covers less area than its own bounding box, and Sentinel Hub reports pixels outside the polygon but inside the bbox-derived request grid as no-data. This means the 50%-valid-pixel threshold used in the original version of this check no longer describes an unusual month; under the corrected data it would flag most of the panel, since a zone's own best month can be as low as ≈43–67% valid depending on its polygon-to-bbox area ratio, not the near-100% typical of the original bounding-box extraction.

I therefore re-ran this check with two lower, more appropriate thresholds (15% and 25% valid-pixel fraction) chosen to flag genuinely extreme outlier months rather than the new, lower-but-normal range. At the 15% threshold, one month is dropped from the primary Kherson-vs-Tulcea panel (Kherson, December 2022, at 7.9% valid — the same month flagged as the worst in the original analysis, though at a much lower valid fraction under the corrected geometry); the re-estimated coefficient is did_term = −0.0733 (HAC p = 0.070), against −0.0747 (HAC p = 0.060) for the full panel — the coefficient is essentially unchanged, but the p-value moves further from, not closer to, conventional significance. At the stricter 25% threshold, two months are dropped and the same pattern holds: did_term = −0.0731 (HAC p = 0.083).

The same test on the four-county panel (Section 4.5) shows the same direction: at the 15% threshold, six zone-months are dropped and the coefficient moves from −0.0661 (HAC p = 0.059) to −0.0479 (HAC p = 0.136); a placebo-in-space re-ranking on the same cleaned data leaves Kherson at the same rank (2nd of 5, one-sided) it holds on the full corrected data. The conclusion of this check is unchanged in spirit from the original version — the primary result is not being propped up by a handful of unusually bad months — but the reason is now less reassuring than before: it is not that low-coverage months are rare and inconsequential, it is that removing them does not rescue an already-marginal result, and if anything makes it slightly weaker. This rules out one specific, benign explanation for why the corrected data weakens the headline result (that a few extremely low-coverage months were dragging it down), without offering a more favourable explanation in its place.

## 5. Discussion

The key methodological innovation of this work is not only the use of satellite data in answering a question of conflict damage, but also the application of the same “falsification” criterion and research context normally used in causal-inference studies generally to identify the lack. It is hardly suppressed “before-after” comparison” anomalies that actually convinced me that the pre-treatment anomaly is also to be expected wherever you make such a comparison. Methodologically naïve “before-after” comparisons of war zones typically aren't going to give a genuinely undisturbed “before” period from which to draw conclusions, and on the contrary, making any such comparisons is likely to blend an impact from an identifiable, dateable, something into the cumulative effect of a combination of war factors.

Legal relevance and its restrictions.

When it comes to environmental harm alone, the existing war crime provision of the Rome Statute that most clearly focuses on this element of the crime, namely Article 8(2)(b)(iv), reads: "Attacks known to cause 'widespread, long-term and severe damage to the natural environment which would be clearly excessive in relation to the concrete and direct overall military advantage anticipated. The results of this study do not address all aspects of that conjunctive-multiple test. The intensity of the vegetation decline (Section 4.2 data) and the extent of the verified flood (Section 4.1 data) gives quantified evidence pertaining specifically to the “severeness” and “widespread” elements. They don't, in themselves, provide the “long-term” element – this study’s NDVI time series only extend into November 2024, an 18-month period after the event, which tells you something but is not necessarily a sign of a lasting, not-reversible change – and they also simply offer no information about the “clearly excessive... military advantage” element, a legal and factual judgment that you can't make from this study's data or scope. This study is not meant to replace a determination; rather, it is provided in support of the facts that would need to be offered. To state the boundary as plainly as possible: this study can establish satellite-observed environmental change and a statistically estimated, event-associated differential. It cannot, on its own, establish who caused that change, their intent or knowledge, the unlawfulness of the act, criminal responsibility, or any of the other elements — beyond severity and extent — that a legal finding of ecocide or a war-crimes determination would require. Following the correction reported in Sections 4.6 and 4.8, it cannot even establish, with confidence, that Kherson's observed decline is statistically distinguishable from independent variation already present in this study's own control panel — a narrower evidentiary claim than an earlier version of this paper suggested, and the one this paper now makes.

Beyond the legal framing, the same findings carry a security dimension. The flooded corridor documented in Section 4.1 and the vegetation loss documented in Section 4.2 sit on agricultural land that fed regional food production and on a river system that supplied downstream water access; a statistically grounded estimate of the scale of that loss is therefore also relevant to food-security and water-security assessments of the region, and to post-war recovery and reconstruction planning, not only to a future prosecution. This connects the present findings to the broader environmental-security and peacebuilding literature (Section 2.4), which has argued for exactly this kind of case-level, quantitative evidence of conflict-attributable environmental harm (Krampe, Kreutz, & Ide, 2025) as a basis for both accountability and recovery planning, rather than treating legal admissibility and security relevance as separate uses of the same underlying evidence.

## 6. Limitations and Threats to Validity

This revision corrects a methodological error in the underlying NDVI extraction — querying each zone's bounding box rather than its true administrative polygon, and filtering clouds only at the scene level rather than per pixel (Section 3.3) — and reports the consequences of that correction plainly rather than only in a changelog. The corrected primary specification (Section 4.2) no longer clears the conventional 5% significance threshold under its own preferred HAC standard errors (p=0.060, versus the originally-reported p=0.022), and the corrected placebo-in-space check (Section 4.6) shows Kherson is no longer the most extreme of the five geographic units in this design, with two of the four Romanian control counties (Brăila, Constanța) independently showing comparable-or-larger shifts of their own (Section 4.8). Every limitation below should be read in light of this: the underlying signal — Kherson's NDVI declining after June 2023 relative to Tulcea — is directionally the same as originally reported, but this design's ability to attribute that decline specifically and confidently to the Kakhovka Dam, rather than to noise already present among only five available geographic units, is weaker than an earlier version of this paper stated.

The narrowed-baseline specification's own placebo test, reported in an earlier version of this paper as failing once HAC-robust standard errors were used, no longer fails under the corrected data (Section 4.3): its HAC p-value is now 0.069, not significant. This specific concern is therefore less severe than previously reported, though the narrowed-baseline specification is still treated only as a secondary illustration of the pre-treatment-quarter problem, not as an independently validated primary result — that role belongs to the primary two-zone specification (Section 4.2), which is the specification most affected by this revision's other findings. The primary specification is supported by one design run, against the Kherson treatment compared to Tulcea, as reported in the main analysis; to cross-check it, the same design is repeated with the four-county panel together (Section 4.5), yielding a similarly-weakened effect (did_term = −0.0661, HAC p = 0.059) supported by two of the four individual control design runs rather than three. This check is not a solution to any outstanding issues, as 5 clusters is the lowest number for which cluster-robust inference is even defined, and even the quarterly event study run on the same panel is not reliably cluster-robust (Section 4.5). No correlate of reservoir water-loss, that is, no reservoir that is comparably collapsing upstream, exists for a large reservoir collapse to be tested causally, and this remains a descriptive rather than an independently causally-tested result.

In addition to the limitations noted above, the following threats to validity were considered (specifically):

- **Selection bias in the control zone.** As with the choice of Tulcea County (Section 3.1), the post-treatment ecological comparability was tested and selection bias in the control area was not assumed. This is not left as an unaddressed concern; it is testable directly (or indirectly) in Section 4.5's four-county panel check, and it remains four human-selected controls rather than an algorithmically weighted synthetic counterfactual, as a Synthetic Control Method design would provide (Section 7). One of the four (Constanța, +0.0186, p=0.602) does not repeat the effect in the pairwise breakdown, and Section 4.8 now traces that specifically to an independent post-event divergence of its own, rather than leaving it a purely open question.
- **Spatial spillover.** The Difference-in-Differences design makes the assumption that there is no impact of the treatment on the control zone. The ~350 km distance between Kherson and the Romanian counties, across an international border, was originally the only argument offered for this assumption. Section 4.8 tests it directly, excluding Kherson and checking whether any control county shifts relative to the others after June 2023: under the corrected data, Tulcea and Galați pass cleanly, but both Brăila and Constanța now show their own significant, independent post-event divergence from the other controls — a larger share of the control panel affected than the original bounding-box analysis found (which flagged only Constanța). The assumption holds for the county used in the primary specification (Tulcea), but not for half of the four-county panel, and Section 4.5's and Section 4.6's results should be read with that in mind.
- **Functional-form sensitivity.** The primary specification models NDVI linearly. Section 4.9 shows the same effect under a log(NDVI) transformation is directionally the same but weaker (p=0.081, versus 0.060 for the linear specification) — under the corrected data, neither functional form clears conventional significance, whereas in the original bounding-box analysis only the log specification failed to. The result is not independent of this modeling choice, and that dependence has grown more consequential under this revision.
- **Data loss due to cloud contamination and the geometry-driven coverage ceiling.** Sentinel-2 optical composites are subject to cloud-driven pixel dropout; this remains true after the correction reported in Section 3.3, but the correction also introduces a separate, structural effect: because a true polygon covers less area than its bounding box, the corrected extraction's ceiling on valid-pixel coverage per zone is substantially lower than the original bounding-box extraction's — as low as ≈43–66% at a zone's best month, depending on its polygon-to-bbox area ratio (verified directly in Section 3.3), not the near-100% the bounding-box version typically returned. Concretely, the worst-affected month in the corrected Kherson series (December 2022) now returns only 7.9% valid pixels, down from 31.4% under the original bounding-box extraction of the same month. Section 4.10 re-runs the low-coverage-month check at thresholds appropriate to this lower ceiling (15% and 25%, rather than the original 50%) and finds that dropping the worst months does not recover the headline result — if anything it weakens slightly further. A further limitation of the current pipeline, now addressed for NDVI but not yet extended elsewhere, is that this polygon+SCL correction has not yet been applied to the flood-extent or true-color imagery pipelines, which still use the original geometry.
- **Monthly aggregation vs. exact event date.** The dam was destroyed on 6 June 2023, but NDVI is aggregated at monthly resolution, so the "June 2023" observation pools 5 pre-event days (1–5 June) together with 25 post-event days into a single monthly value; the June coefficient should be read as a monthly-period effect, not the instantaneous response to 6 June specifically. In a simple, immediate-shock setting this kind of coarsening would be expected to dilute rather than inflate the post-treatment signal, biasing an estimate toward zero rather than away from it — but this is a general expectation, not something asserted here as guaranteed in a setting with pre-existing conflict dynamics and a multi-week flood recession (Section 4.1), so it is offered as the likely direction, not a certainty.
- **Serial correlation.** This is corrected directly in the Newey-West HAC standard errors of the regression coefficients (Section 3.3, 4.4 and 4.5), instead of being ignored.
- **Few-cluster inference.** The few-cluster-inference concern applies to only five clusters, well below the 30-40+ rule of thumb, and even at that cluster count the cluster-robust standard errors are numerically degenerate in the quarterly event study (Section 4.5), which is why HAC is reported as the primary specification there. Section 4.6 quantifies this concern directly with a placebo-in-space / randomization-inference check: across the five possible unit-assignments, Kherson's own effect is no longer the most extreme under the corrected data — it ranks 2nd of 5 one-sided (exact p=0.40) or 3rd of 5 two-sided (exact p=0.60), with Brăila and Constanța showing comparable or larger effects of their own. This is now the paper's most direct piece of evidence that the primary result cannot be confidently distinguished, under this design, from variation already present in the control panel (Section 4.8) — a ceiling on inferential confidence that no amount of additional modeling can overcome without more clusters (Section 7).
- **Multiple testing in the event study.** The quarterly event study (Section 4.3) tests eleven coefficients against one reference quarter. Section 4.7 reports what survives Bonferroni and Benjamini–Hochberg correction for that multiplicity — under the corrected data, more quarters survive than under the original bounding-box analysis, a rare instance where this specific check strengthened rather than weakened, and one that should not be read as generalizing to the paper's other, weakened checks.
- **Single-index measurement.** NDVI has a narrower scope of vegetation greenness without direct measurement of soil salinization, water contamination or other aspects of environmental damage that could be relevant to a more comprehensive assessment of damage (Section 7).

## 7. Future Work

A number of extensions were considered useful but unable to be developed within the scope and timeframe of this study and are therefore noted here, but not pursued:

- **Understanding the independent Brăila and Constanța divergence.** Section 4.8 establishes that two of the four Romanian control counties show their own statistically detectable post-June-2023 shift, unrelated to the Kakhovka Dam, but does not identify a cause. A land-cover classification, disrupted Black Sea shipping and agricultural-market data, or a SAR-based soil-moisture/salinization time series (see below) for these two counties specifically would help determine whether this is a shared regional phenomenon, two unrelated local ones, or a remaining artifact of the extraction pipeline not yet identified.
- **A genuinely larger control panel.** The placebo-in-space check (Section 4.6) is directly limited by having only five geographic units to rank against — the best possible exact randomization p-value obtainable is mechanically 1/5 = 0.20, and this design's actual result (0.40–0.60) is now well short of even that ceiling. Expanding the control panel beyond the four Romanian counties — for example into the non-combatant zone of the Black Sea/Danube basin more broadly, such as Bulgarian and Moldovan counterparts — would both reduce the variance in the pooled estimate and give a substantially less coarse randomization-inference distribution than five equally-likely ranks. This item has become more urgent, not less, following this revision's findings.
- **Synthetic Control Method (SCM).** This study's four-county panel (Section 4.5) selects candidate regions by hand. An algorithmically weighted synthetic counterfactual, built from a larger candidate pool (see above), would directly address why Brăila and Constanța behave differently from Tulcea and Galați, rather than treating that difference as a limitation of a small, hand-picked panel.
- **Additional vegetation and moisture indices.** Re-processing the causal model with the EVI (Enhanced Vegetation Index) (insensitive to soil background) and NDMI (Normalized Difference Moisture Index) would help assess whether the result found on NDVI is robust to the choice of index, or partly an artifact of it.
- **Meteorological and soil covariates.** These would help disentangle the conflict signal from intra-month variability not already absorbed by the monthly fixed effects, and could help explain the independent Brăila/Constanța divergence noted above.
- **SAR-based soil moisture and salinization analysis.** Salinization and soil-moisture regime changes can be expressed in time series with Sentinel-1 C-band SAR (VV/VH ratio), a direction of damage information not captured by NDVI, and a plausible tool for investigating the Brăila/Constanța divergence directly.
- **Extending the polygon+SCL correction to the flood-extent and true-color pipelines.** This revision applies the polygon-geometry and pixel-level cloud-masking correction (Section 3.3) to the NDVI pipeline only. The flood-extent (Section 4.1) and true-color imagery pipelines have not yet been re-verified against the same standard.
- **Deeper engagement with evidentiary-admissibility legal scholarship.** A more thorough exploration of standards relating to the admissibility of scientific evidence, such as the famous ‘Daubert’ standards and the equivalents in international tribunals, would bolster the paper's focus on the law–statistics divide.

## 8. Conclusion

This study applies a Difference-in-Differences/falsification approach — not previously demonstrated for this event — to satellite-derived vegetation data, and this version of the paper corrects a methodological error in the underlying data extraction (querying bounding boxes rather than true administrative polygons, and filtering cloud contamination only at the scene level) that an earlier version of this analysis did not catch. Reporting that correction, and its consequences, honestly is itself part of what this paper is trying to demonstrate: that a statistically sound method for documenting war-time environmental harm has to survive being checked, not just look clean on first pass.

The corrected primary specification estimates a directionally-consistent NDVI decline in Kherson relative to Tulcea (−0.0747, HAC p = 0.060) that no longer clears the conventional 5% significance threshold, though it remains close to it, and a broad-window placebo test at a counterfactual pre-event date still returns a clean, non-significant result. The four-county panel (Section 4.5) shows the same directional weakening, and two of its four individual controls (Galați, Brăila) still show significant effects of their own size and direction, while Tulcea is now marginal and Constanța remains null. The most consequential finding of this revision is the exact randomization-inference check (Section 4.6): with the corrected data, Kherson is no longer the most extreme of the five geographic units available to this design — Brăila and Constanța both show comparable-or-larger shifts under the identical placebo-in-space procedure — and a direct spillover check with Kherson excluded entirely (Section 4.8) confirms both counties have their own independent post-event divergence, for reasons this design does not identify. I checked, and can rule out, one benign explanation for this weakening — a handful of especially low-coverage months driving the result (Section 4.10) — it is not that.

The honest summary is narrower than the one an earlier version of this paper reported: Kherson's NDVI shows a real, directionally consistent decline after the Kakhovka Dam's destruction relative to its main control zone, but with only five geographic units available, this design cannot establish that decline as statistically distinguishable from the independent variation already present among the control zones themselves. This is not a failure of the causal-inference approach as a method — the same falsification apparatus (placebo tests, randomization inference, spillover checks) that established the original, more confident-sounding result is what surfaced this weaker, more honest one — but it is a genuine limit on what this specific case, with this specific, still-small control panel, can currently support. The paper's contribution to the broader literature on satellite-based conflict-damage assessment and the legal frameworks for environmental crime is, if anything, sharpened by this: rigorous, statistically explicit methods should be expected to sometimes weaken an initially promising result once checked more thoroughly, and reporting that outcome plainly — rather than only reporting the version of the analysis that clears significance — is exactly the kind of standardization this literature has been calling for.
